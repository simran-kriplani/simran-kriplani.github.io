Applied Behavioural Strategy in Campus Leadership

SYNAPSE UofT · Psychological Health Society

Campaign strategy Content strategy Behavioural design Brand voice

Campus organizations often face the same challenge: how do you move students from awareness to action when attention is fragmented and the audience is already overwhelmed? This problem became a practical behavioural design exercise in the leadership roles I held across student organizations.

At SYNAPSE, I helped lead communications that made neuroscience more accessible and relevant to a broad campus audience. The work involved reducing complexity, creating clearer sequencing in messaging, and making calls to action feel smaller and more achievable. That is where behavioural thinking becomes particularly useful: people are more likely to act when action feels easy, emotionally safe, and aligned with a clear identity.

At the Psychological Health Society, I focused on building campaigns that normalized help-seeking and reframed mental health conversations around attainable habits rather than abstract ideals. Instead of pushing a single dramatic call to action, the strategy centered on consistency, repetition, and message clarity. The same principle held across both roles: a strong brand voice and a coherent content strategy create a better behavioural environment than any one-off campaign ever could.

What made this work especially meaningful was seeing how simple strategic decisions—tone, timing, framing, and content structure—shaped how people responded. The result was not just better engagement, but a more intentional pathway from interest to action.
