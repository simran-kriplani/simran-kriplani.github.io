Who’s Really In Charge?

Competence and Control in the age of AI

Literature synthesis GenAI-assisted research Behavioural frameworks Academic & technical writing

Artificial intelligence (AI) has moved from the background of consumer life to the center of everyday decision-making. Consumers now depend on AI to choose products, plan travel, manage finances, and navigate health and career decisions. Simultaneously, AI interfaces are able to communicate through natural language, offer personalized recommendations, and present themselves in distinctly humanlike ways. These features encourage consumers to engage with AI not merely as a tool, but as a social actor capable of offering guidance, exercising judgment, and influencing choice. As AI becomes increasingly embedded in consequential decisions, a central question emerges: when AI takes on socially meaningful attributes, how do consumers negotiate resistance, reliance, and control?

Consider two otherwise identical systems. One is introduced as an assistant. The other is introduced as an advisor. Although these labels may appear superficial at first, they carry strong social meaning. Decades of research in psychology and consumer behaviour demonstrates that individuals rely heavily on social role schemas to interpret others’ intentions, competence, and power. In other words, we naturally interpret humanlike technologies through the same social frameworks we use to understand people. We assign them roles—assistant, advisor, expert, partner—and those roles carry expectations about authority and power. In this case, an assistant label suggests that you are in charge. The AI is there to help, respond to your instructions, and make your decisions easier. An advisor label suggests something different: expertise, judgment, and a willingness to guide you toward what it considers the better choice. These interpretations become especially crucial in situations marked by uncertainty, limited knowledge, or high stakes, where consumers are more likely to rely on external sources. In such contexts, subtle cues about competence and control can determine whether users feel empowered or constrained, confident or dependent.

Rather than reflecting a fixed opposition between competence and control, the same role cues can give rise to different patterns of inference depending on how users interpret the AI’s role in the interaction. Framing AI as knowledgeable or expert can enhance perceived competence and increase users’ willingness to rely on its recommendations; however, this may diminish consumers’ sense of autonomy or create discomfort when decision authority appears to shift from the user to the machine. Framing AI as a subordinate, on the other hand, often enhances perceived user control and agency, but may also lead users to question the system’s competence and credibility. Interestingly, a user’s familiarity with the domain in which the AI operates may critically moderate how role framing is perceived.

When consumers lack domain knowledge or experience, they are more vulnerable to feelings of uncertainty and dependency, making them particularly sensitive to signals of authority and dominance. Imagine a novice investor engaging with an AI system introduced as a financial advisor. On the surface, the advisor framing should be reassuring since it signals precisely the expertise the user lacks, but in practice, it often works against them. Without sufficient background to evaluate the system's reasoning, the user cannot verify, question, or push back on what they are told; In such cases, an AI advisor may appear intimidating or disempowering, leading to loss of control. By contrast, when consumers possess high familiarity with a domain, they may feel better equipped to evaluate, challenge, or contextualize AI recommendations. For these users, advisor positioning may be experienced not as a threat, but as a valuable source of expertise that complements their own knowledge.

There is, then, no single correct way to frame an AI system; a default persona applied uniformly across users will always be miscalibrated for someone, too authoritative for some and too deferential for others. This shows that when AI takes on socially meaningful attributes, users are not just evaluating a technology, they are negotiating a division of responsibility: What should the AI do? What should I do? And who gets the final say? As AI moves further into decisions that affect our money, health, work, and everyday lives, those questions may become just as important as the technology itself.
