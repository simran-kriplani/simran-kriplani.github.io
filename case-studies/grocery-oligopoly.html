A Consumer Behaviour Perspective on Canada's Grocery Oligopoly

Independent research project · University of Toronto

Quantitative analysis Pricing strategy Market structure Executive presentations Python & Excel

Canada's grocery sector is characterized by a concentrated mix of large retail chains, each strategically operating a portfolio of banners that cover discount, mid-market, and premium segments. This structure allows the same parent company to appear competitive across categories while maintaining a high degree of control over which products, prices, and brands customers encounter first.

My analysis focused on how pricing patterns moved across banners, time periods, and product categories. By combining Python-enabled data cleaning with Excel-based analysis, I traced where price changes were synchronized across competitors and where firms appeared to segment by consumer value proposition rather than by pure direct competition.

The key insight was that the market does not behave like a set of independent merchants competing fiercely in a single category. Instead, it behaves more like a coordinated portfolio strategy: premium banners protect margin while discount banners defend volume, and nearby banners often react in ways that reinforce a broader market structure rather than simply compete on an individual basis.

From a behavioural and strategic lens, this matters because consumer decisions are shaped not only by absolute price, but by the overlay of comparison, perception, and brand architecture. When shoppers are exposed to multiple banners across a single retailer, they are not simply choosing between products; they are evaluating a perceived market landscape shaped by the retailer's strategic framing.

I translated the findings into a client-facing research memo and presentation that connected the data to commercial interpretation, giving a clear view of how market power is exercised through pricing architecture, portfolio design, and brand segmentation.
