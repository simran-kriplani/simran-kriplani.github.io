Look Good, Feel Good?

Using Behaviour Change Strategies to Dress with Intention

Behavioural Intervention Enclothed Cognition Identity & Self-concept

I. Introduction and Background

Confidence is a trait that shifts with context, cues, and the way we prepare ourselves to navigate the day. One pattern I noticed over the past year was how strongly my confidence correlated with something I always dismissed as superficial: how I dressed in the morning. When I defaulted to wearing what was quickest and easiest, I moved through campus quietly and on autopilot; When I curated my outfits, I carried myself with more clarity, assertiveness, and a sense of readiness that felt disproportionate to such a small behaviour. Presenting myself in a way that aligned with the identity I wanted - competent, organized, and self-assured - was more significant than I initially recognized.

Enclothed cognition, described by Adam and Galinsky (2012), is the idea that the clothes we wear can affect our mental processes and emotional state. It is fascinating how putting on a certain outfit can shape how we think, feel, and act. My goal was neither to become more fashionable, nor to automate personal style, but to make the process of dressing intentionally low-friction enough to become reliable. Past attempts to “dress better” were vague and usually collapsed under the weight of rushed mornings, cluttered closets, and decision fatigue, which was cognitively exhausting and left no room for thoughtful decisions (Baumeister et al., 1998). Despite the dissonance, default clothing became the path of least resistance, and quietly undermined the confidence I wanted to carry.

Unlike habits aimed at productivity or fitness, I chose dressing with purpose because it consistently influenced a broader outcome I care deeply about: self-confidence. Moreover, it is specific and observable, making it ideal for testing behavioural science strategies. Drawing on literature about identity-based motivation, implementation intentions, and environment design, this paper describes my experience and attempts to dress more intentionally, transforming a previously challenging behaviour into a structured routine that can meaningfully impact my self-confidence.

II. Behaviour Interventions

The first strategy I implemented was reframing my behaviour from merely “picking outfits” to “becoming a person who shows up confidently.” Thinking about clothing beyond its utilitarian value reinforced it as a form of self-expression rather than an aesthetic choice. This premise is supported by identity-based motivation theory, which posits that behaviours are more likely to be initiated and sustained when they are congruent with one’s active self-concept and perceived identity (Oyserman, 2015; Clear, 2018). Thus, a shift in mindset was the primary step to ensure my actions were a natural reflection of my desired self-image.

This psychological effect is further explained by research on enclothed cognition, which demonstrates that clothing carries symbolic meaning and can influence cognitive performance when the wearer attributes significance to it (Adam & Galinsky, 2012). For example, participants who wore a lab coat described as a doctor’s coat performed better on attention-based tasks than those wearing an identical coat labeled as a painter’s smock. In practice, when I dressed intentionally, I noticed a sense of clarity and composure that extended beyond appearance and into posture, focus, and confidence. Rather than viewing clothing as cosmetic, I treated it as a behavioural cue, one that consistently primed me into a more self-directed mindset.

I paired this identity-based motivation with structured planning. I implemented habit stacking by linking outfit selection to an existing nighttime routine: after brushing my teeth, I immediately chose and laid out the next day’s clothing. This approach draws on work by Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) showing that implementation intentions, explicit “if-then” plans specifying when and how actions will occur, significantly increase behavioural follow-through by automating cues for initiation. Creating mental associations between brushing teeth and choosing an outfit even made it easier to remember (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006; Clear, 2018).

Furthermore, this activity reduced reliance on decision-making in the morning. Baumeister et al. (1998) show that repeated decisions, even minor ones, degrade cognitive control and increase the likelihood of default behaviour; Prior to using implementation intentions, my mornings were filled with indecision that often resulted in convenience-based choices. Once outfit coordination became procedurally fixed, dressing well became automatic rather than effortful. Subjectively, I experienced reduced morning stress and greater consistency, even on days when motivation was low.

Finally, I redesigned my physical environment to reduce friction and increase behavioural visibility. Research in behavioural economics demonstrates that behaviour is strongly shaped by contextual cues, often more powerfully than by intention alone (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). Accordingly, I reorganized my wardrobe to make preferred items accessible and salient, removed clothing that did not align my identity goal, and stored complete outfits together. This eliminated ambiguity and decreased the effort required to follow through. Over time, my environment functioned as a silent guide, making intentional dressing the easiest available option rather than an act of deliberation. By aligning identity, planning, and environment, I shifted intentional dressing from a far-fetched goal into a stable, self-reinforcing routine.

III. Measurement and Outcomes

Using a combination of behavioural metrics and subjective measures, I evaluated both the development of habit automaticity and its psychological impact. The most fundamental behavioural measure was whether I prepared an outfit the night before (recorded as yes/no). This metric directly indexed behavioural follow-through and served as a manipulation check for whether the implementation intentions and habit stacking was actually executed. I observed that the night-before preparation became increasingly consistent and easy, indicating that the planning behaviour was successfully integrated into my routine.

To assess habit automaticity, I measured the time it took to get my outfit ready. Automaticity is commonly defined as a decrease in cognitive involvement as a behaviour becomes habitual (Lally et al., 2010). As habits strengthen, they become faster and less effortful. Therefore, reductions in preparation time could indicate increasing automation. Across weeks, my preparation time decreased and stabilized, suggesting the emergence of a routine that no longer required conscious effort to initiate. However, faster preparation alone is an ambiguous signal. Speed could reflect a functional, automated routine or conversely represent disengagement, such as reaching for the same item without intention. To account for this, I tracked outfit variance as a moderator. A daily log recorded whether I wore meaningfully different combinations rather than defaulting to a uniform. Since my preparation time decreased and variation remained high, there was strong evidence that automation stemmed from effective habit formation rather than reduced effort or convenience. In this way, outfit variance functioned as a conceptual safeguard, distinguishing genuine automaticity from behavioural shortcutting.

Confidence was assessed using a daily self-report rating from 1 (very low) to 10 (very high). While imperfect, single-item scales can capture global self-evaluations reliably when the construct is measured frequently. My confidence ratings showed an upward trend, rising from an average of 4/10 to 8/10 over a few weeks: I felt reliably more prepared and less hesitant during social interactions. Importantly, these ratings captured how equipped and capable I felt on that given day, rather than personality-level change in confidence, which typically requires structured, long-term behavioural interventions (Lally et al., 2010).

As a bonus, I recorded the number of social interactions I initiated daily (e.g., starting conversations, participating in class). Confidence is often described abstractly, but its practical expression is behavioural. This metric was included, though recorded conservatively and noted as relatively stable, as a real-world signal that perceived confidence can translate into action.

Together, this design reduced reliance on narrative alone and allowed subjective experience to be evaluated against observable behaviour. Rather than claiming certainty, it provided structured evidence that a small, identity-aligned action could influence psychological functioning and everyday behaviour in measurable ways.

Limitations

Nevertheless, several limitations persist. Self-report measures are vulnerable to expectancy effects: believing the intervention should work may have inflated my confidence ratings. The novelty of the intervention may have temporarily boosted motivation as well; Sustained effects cannot be confidently inferred without long-term measurement. Moreover, there was no measurement of a baseline, making it difficult to quantify change relative to pre-intervention outcomes. Without baseline measurement, this change is directional rather than causal. If I were to repeat this project, I would extend the measurement window, introduce a baseline phase, and implement a simple reward structure to reinforce consistency. I would also incorporate external feedback (e.g., peer ratings of presence or composure) to further reduce reliance on self-report.

Reflection & Conclusion

Ultimately, what began as a superficial concern became an experiment in how identity, environment, and intention shape behaviour. Dressing well did not create confidence, but it consistently invited it by changing how I showed up to my day. I learned that lasting change is less about forcing discipline and more about designing systems that make the right behaviour easier than the wrong one. Through behaviour interventions, my habit became attainable rather than aspirational, reminding me that my goals can be reached through small choices that compound into meaningful psychological shifts.
