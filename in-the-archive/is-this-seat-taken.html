Is This Seat Taken?

A Behavioural Fix for Crowded Libraries

Nudges & Choice Architecture Behavioural Design Decision-Making

During peak exam periods at the University of Toronto, library spaces fill up fast as students compete for limited seating. A common pattern emerges: students enter crowded libraries without knowing where space is available, wander through multiple floors searching for seats, and often cluster in high-traffic areas while deciding where to settle. This behaviour leads to unnecessary congestion, wasted time, and inefficient use of available study space. Importantly, this is not due to a lack of effort or insufficient seating. Rather, it reflects predictable challenges in decision-making under uncertainty and time pressure. The problem, in other words, is one of distribution rather than scarcity.

Students who crowd entry points slow down everyone behind them, while noise and movement disrupt those already working. At the same time, underused floors represent real institutional waste. These behaviours are shaped by psychological mechanisms and amplified by the environment.

Libraries provide little to no real-time visibility into occupancy, forcing students into inefficient search patterns. Students face high search costs and must rely on trial-and-error, moving from one area to another in hopes of finding an open spot. Entry points are often unstructured, with little guidance on where to go, creating bottlenecks. Students also experience a sense of urgency; with exams approaching, the priority becomes securing a seat quickly, even if that means entering a space that appears full rather than considering alternatives. Inside, there are limited directional cues to guide movement toward less crowded areas, and students disproportionately rely on what is most salient and immediately visible.

Perception is also highly local. A crowded entrance or a full first floor can create the impression that the entire building is at capacity, even when other areas remain underutilized. Herding behaviour reinforces this effect: students tend to follow visible crowds or remain in familiar areas, further concentrating demand in already busy spaces. As a result, the environment fails to support efficient decision-making at critical moments: before entering, while navigating, and when deciding whether to continue searching or settle.

To address this, I propose three complementary, low-cost interventions that target different stages of the decision process while preserving students’ freedom of choice. The first is the introduction of real-time capacity dashboards at library entrances and through a mobile interface. These displays would show occupancy levels by floor or zone using simple, colour-coded indicators (e.g., green for low occupancy, red for near capacity), along with suggestions for alternative locations. A student approaching the library could immediately see that one floor is nearly full while another has ample space, or that a nearby building offers better availability. This kind of information reduces uncertainty at the point of entry, allowing students to make more informed decisions before they commit. It would also help distribute students more evenly across available spaces and avoid unnecessary entry into already crowded areas.

The second intervention focuses on behaviour once students are inside the library, where search inefficiencies and congestion are most pronounced. This involves implementing a system of directed-flow architecture: subtle environmental cues that guide movement toward less crowded areas. For example, directional signage at key decision points, such as staircases or hallway intersections, could highlight where seating is available (“Quiet study → Floor 4, moderate availability”), while floor markings or visual cues could reinforce these pathways. By making certain options more visible and easier to follow, the design creates a natural path of least resistance. When people are already juggling multiple decisions—where to sit, what to study, how much time they have—they are especially likely to follow these cues. In effect, the environment does some of the cognitive work for them, reducing the need for trial-and-error searching and easing pressure on congested zones.

The third intervention addresses what happens after students have secured a seat, specifically the tendency to occupy study spaces even when no longer actively studying. Many students remain seated while using their phones, taking extended breaks, or engaging in conversation, reducing turnover and exacerbating scarcity. To address this, the library can introduce designated break zones: comfortable and clearly marked areas intended for relaxation and conversation, paired with gentle exit prompts within study areas. For instance, signage might read, “Finished studying? Take a break in the lounge downstairs,” or “Need to talk? Collaboration spaces are available on Floor 1.” This intervention works by offering a clear and attractive alternative to staying seated, reducing the inertia associated with remaining in place. By encouraging students to relocate when they are no longer actively studying, the system increases seat turnover without imposing restrictions.

Together, these interventions address the seat problem at three distinct stages: before entry, during search, and after settling. Each one works by giving students better information or a more obvious path at the moment they are deciding what to do. By redesigning these environments to better align with how people actually think and decide, the university can improve both efficiency and user experience. In this way, relatively simple changes have the potential to transform a chaotic search process into a smoother, more coordinated system.
