When the Ladder Becomes the Snake

What happens to motivation when effort stops predicting outcomes

In a children's board game where you climb ladders and slide down snakes, the appeal rests on one assumption: progress and setback are both possible, and which one you get is mostly chance. Real economic mobility has worked a little differently — not pure chance, but not pure effort either, more like a board where ladders have historically outnumbered snakes just enough to make the promise worth believing. Work hard, follow the rules, and things tilt in your favour over time. That promise is less a fact and more a psychological contract between a person and the system they live in, about what effort is supposed to buy. A growing body of research and policy forecasting suggests that contract is secretly being rewritten.

A Policy Horizons Canada report put the shift in stark terms: by 2040, downward mobility may become the norm, reshaping motivation, mental health, and trust in institutions along the way. The mechanisms are concrete. Housing, once a milestone you worked toward, is becoming the main bottleneck — ownership increasingly determined by what your family can hand down rather than what you can earn, nudging the economy from meritocracy toward something closer to neo-aristocracy. Post-secondary education, the other traditional ladder, is losing its signaling power in the same stretch — a degree reliably converting into income and status used to be close to a given, and that conversion is weakening, especially for people without existing capital to wait it out.

None of this would be nearly as corrosive if people simply gave up on the ladder. The harder problem is what happens when the belief doesn't fully die, just gets contradicted by lived experience; people are still told to aim high and work hard, while sensing the system underneath no longer holds up its end. That gap has a name worth remembering: aspirational dissonance, the friction between an internalized expectation of progress and a reality that isn't delivering it. Unlike ordinary disappointment, which resolves once you lower your expectations, this version persists precisely because the expectation keeps getting reinforced from the outside — by family, culture, and the entire architecture of "just work hard" advice. The result? Chronic frustration, the particular exhaustion of running hard on a treadmill you're told is actually a ladder.

The deeper unease here echoes an argument made three decades ago. After the Cold War, Francis Fukuyama argued in The End of History that liberal democracy had outlasted its ideological rivals, not because it was perfect, but because nothing else credibly offered freedom, prosperity, and recognition at once. His warning concerned what follows that victory: a society that meets nearly all material needs may produce "last men," people who are comfortable but aimless, with no struggle left to give their effort meaning. Upward mobility may have been quietly filling that gap, giving ordinary striving a visible direction. Remove it, and people are left with neither security nor a sense of purpose.

This is why the question is not only economic. Motivation is usually described as a personal trait, but much of it rests on a belief rarely examined: that effort and outcome are reliably connected. When that link weakens, people do not simply become less driven. They lose the framework that told them what their effort was for. The policy question is how to rebuild access to housing and education. The psychological question is harder to tackle: what sustains a sense of progress when the old markers of it can no longer be assumed?
