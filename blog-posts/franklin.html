We Didn't Build the House We Live In

The systems we inhabit and the choices that created them

We talk about technology as if it has a will of its own.

The algorithm decided. AI is taking jobs. The internet changed everything.

In each sentence, a machine is the one acting, while the people who designed, funded, and deployed it disappear from our view. Historian Leo Marx argued that the word technology has become so broad that it presents itself as an independent force driving history, rather than as the product of political, economic, and social choices. The consequences extend beyond semantics: when technological change is framed as inevitable, the decisions behind it become harder to question. There is little room to debate who benefits, who bears the costs, or whether a different direction was possible. What appears to be technological progress can therefore become a convenient way to absolve people of responsibility.

In The Real World of Technology, Ursula Franklin describes technology as the house we all live in: a structure that influences not only the tools we use, but how we work, organize institutions, and relate to one another. She distinguishes between holistic technologies, in which individuals retain control over an entire process and exercise their own judgment, and prescriptive technologies, which divide work into standardized tasks governed by someone else's instructions. A potter shapes a piece from beginning to end; an assembly-line worker performs one step in a process designed elsewhere. The distinction is not between traditional and modern tools, or good and bad technology, but where control resides when efficiency and standardization become the organizing principles. Franklin extends this criticism to institutions such as education and healthcare, where production models that prioritize measurable output and uniform results can displace growth models that value individual development, relationships, and judgment. When everything is organized around what can be measured and optimized, what cannot be easily quantified risks becoming less valuable.

The tension becomes particularly interesting in the digital world, where participation can easily be mistaken for control. Social media platforms, for instance, give people the ability to create, share, and form conversations. They appear to offer holistic participation by placing creative tools in individuals' hands rather than restricting them to predefined roles. Yet the platforms themselves determine what becomes visible, how engagement is measured, which voices are amplified, and how personal data is used. Users may create the content, but they rarely control the systems that distribute it. This complicates Franklin's distinction without making it less relevant. A technology can enable individual expression while remaining prescriptive at the structural level. Access does not necessarily confer agency, and participation does not guarantee a say in how its conditions are designed.

Franklin's critique also raises a question of whose knowledge and contributions are truly valued. Her feminist perspective draws attention to how technological and institutional structures can privilege certain forms of expertise while marginalizing others. Her metaphor of the scientific sandbox captures this imbalance: women may be permitted to participate, but only within existing rules and with established tools, rather than having the authority to challenge those rules or build something different. This goes beyond representation; it is about who gets to define what counts as knowledge, whose judgment is trusted, and whose priorities shape the institutions being built. The same questions matter when we consider the development of AI and algorithmic systems. The data selected, objectives optimized, and values embedded in these systems reflect the assumptions and priorities of the people and institutions behind them. When those perspectives go unquestioned, existing inequalities can be reproduced under the appearance of objective, automated decision-making.

Together, Franklin and Marx offer a way of understanding the technological world we have constructed. Franklin shows how technology shapes the conditions of everyday life; Marx reminds us that those conditions were shaped by human decisions in the first place. The house was built, its rules established, and its design served particular interests. Yet the more we describe technology as an autonomous force, the easier it becomes to forget that any of this was decided. The question, then, is not simply whether a technology is innovative, efficient, or capable of something new. It is who decides what it should do, whose interests it serves, whose knowledge determines its design, and importantly, who has the power to challenge it.
