One Size Does NOT Fit All

Rethinking the Cultural Boundaries of the Neuropsychological Testing

In an increasingly diverse society, the question of who neuropsychological tests are designed for is no longer a peripheral concern, but a central one. Most neuropsychological assessments have been standardized on WEIRD populations, those who are Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic, despite being administered to individuals from a wide range of cultural, linguistic, and educational backgrounds. This mismatch raises urgent ethical and clinical concerns: What happens when diagnostic tools do not reflect the realities of the people they assess?

Culture refers to attitudes, behaviours, and beliefs that are shared by a certain population. While basic cognitive processes are common across all human beings, these functions can develop and manifest in culturally-distinctive ways, impacting symptom presentation, test performance, and ultimately shaping diagnosis and treatment. For instance, cultural norms related to social hierarchy and stigma may shape how psychological injuries and mental states are presented. Ramani et al. (2024) found that Indian and Chinese individuals are more likely to somaticize psychological distress, which may be misinterpreted by Western diagnostic frameworks as physical ailments rather than mental health issues. As Merkley et al. (2022) emphasize, what is often treated as objective measurement may, in fact, be a reflection of cultural assumptions. These assumptions built into test content, structure, and interpretation can lead to misdiagnosis, underdiagnosis, or inappropriate interventions, particularly for racialized, immigrant, or bilingual clients.

Since neuropsychological tools were constructed under Western beliefs, they lack the cultural context required to accurately represent cognitive functioning across diverse populations, often relying on test-taking behaviors that may not align with the learning styles of non-Western populations. Without construct equivalence, poor test performance may reflect unfamiliarity with test content or administration rather than true cognitive deficits. For example, a bilingual client may underperform on a verbal task not because of language difficulty, but due to test instructions or content that do not match their cultural schema. Without adjusting for these disparities, clinicians risk drawing flawed conclusions and perpetuating systemic inequities in care.

Cultural disparities in neuropsychological testing undermine both the validity of results and the fairness of clinical judgments. Many tests, such as the Trail Making Test, carry implicit biases that disproportionately disadvantage clients with limited formal education or non-Western concepts of intelligence, placing them at risk of being misclassified as cognitively impaired. Moreover, ecological validity, or how well test results predict real-world functioning, varies significantly across cultural settings, which further challenges the universality of cognitive interpretations. As such, ethical neuropsychological assessment demands not only technical skill, but cultural humility, contextual insight, and a commitment to justice. Despite these challenges, there is growing evidence that culturally adapted interventions, such as CA-CBT, improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic engagement, underscoring the need for assessments that also reflect cultural values and norms. Emerging tools like the Multicultural Neuropsychological Scale and the ECLECTIC framework* represent important strides toward inclusive, equity-based assessment as well; however, culture is a dynamic construct, shaped by migration, language, and socioeconomic factors, which makes it difficult to define or control for in research and clinical practice. Thus, the future of neuropsychological testing must prioritize cultural adaptability as a foundational design principle, ensuring both scientific rigor and ethical responsibility.

Ultimately, culture is foundational to how cognition is expressed, measured, and interpreted. Given the increasing cultural and linguistic diversity among clients, outdated norms and “one-size-fits-all” tools pose serious risks to both diagnosis and care. Future directions must include development of culturally centered assessments, improved clinician training, and greater empathy and awareness of how cultural context shapes cognitive functioning. Only by embedding cultural responsiveness into every stage of assessment can neuropsychology develop and uphold frameworks that are as diverse as the populations they serve.

*ECLECTIC Framework: a tool for culturally contextualized neuropsychological and psychological evaluations by taking into account factors such as Education, Culture, Language, Economics, Communication, Testing situation, Intelligence conceptualization, and Context of immigration.
